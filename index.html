THREADS AT RISK

A Digital Archive of Declining Indian Textile Traditions

About

Threads at Risk is a digital archive documenting traditional Indian textile practices that are declining, transforming, or in need of safeguarding. The archive brings together textiles from different regions of India, documenting their materials, techniques, motifs, communities, histories, and contemporary status.

Rather than presenting textiles only as beautiful objects, the archive traces how these traditions have changed over time due to factors such as the decline of traditional patronage, industrial production, changing markets, and changing consumer practices. Through structured metadata and digital documentation, the project aims to preserve information about these textile traditions and make it more accessible to students, researchers, and the wider public.

KHES WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
NORTHWESTERN INDIA, TRADITIONALLY ASSOCIATED WITH PUNJAB & PANIPAT , HARYANA, INDIA
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
TRADITIONAL WEAVERS OF PUNJAB-PANIPAT REGION
TEXTILE TYPE
HANDWOVEN DOUBLE CLOTH TEXTILE
MATERIAL
TRADITIONALLY COTTON, SILK WAS USED
TECHNIQUE
TRADITIONALLY COTTON, SILK WAS USED
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
GEOMETRIC AND CHECKED PATTERNS
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
KHES DEVELOPED SEVERAL CENTURIES AGO UNDER MUGHAL RULE WHEN COTTON BLANKETS WERE IMPORTANT ARTICLES OF USE AND TRADE.
TRANSFORMATION
TRADITIONAL KHES WAS WOVEN ON SMALLER LOOMS AND LARGER PIECES COULD BE ASSEMBLED FROM MULTIPLE WIDTHS. CONTEMPORARY PRODUCTION ALSO USES JACQUARD LOOMS FOR FIGURATIVE DESIGNS AND PATTERNS.
CURRENT STATUS
NEEDS SAFEGUARDING / DECLINING TRADITIONAL PRACTICE
THREATS/CHALLENGES
DECLINE OF TRADITIONAL HOUSEHOLD WEAVING, CHANGES IN PRODUCTION TECHNOLOGY, CHANGING MARKETS AND THE SHIFT FROM TRADITIONAL GEOMETRIC KHES TO NEWER MACHINE JACQUARD PRODUCED DESIGNS

REGION OF ORIGIN
CHAMBAL VALLEY, HIMACHAL PRADESH, INDIA
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
TRADITIONAL EMBROIDERERS AND ARTISANS OF THE CHAMBA REGION
TEXTILE TYPE
EMBROIDERED, PICTORIAL EMBROIDERERS AND ARTISANS OF THE CHAMBA REGION
MATERIAL
FINE MUSLIN, KHADDAR, COTTON, SILK
TECHNIQUE
DORUKHA (DOUBLE SIDED SATIN STITCH) PRODUCING A SIMILAR DESIGN ON BOTH SIDES OF THE FABRIC

LOTUS, CYPRESS, TREES, PEACOCKS, PARROTS, DEER, FLORAL DESIGNS, MYTHOLOGICAL AND NARRATIVE SCENES

CURRENT STATUS
IDENTIFIED AS AN ENDANGERED CRAFTS REQUIRES SAFEGUARDING
THREATS/CHALLENGES
DECLINE OF TRADITIONAL PATRONAGE REDUCED TRANSMISSION OF TRADITIONAL SKILLS, CHANGING MARKETS, AND THE CHALLENGE OF MAINTAINING TRADITIONAL TECHNIQUES WHILE ADAPTING TO CONTEMPORARY DEMAND.

THIGMA

REGION OF ORIGIN
LADAKH, PARTICULARLY NUBRA VALLEY AND SABU
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
LADAKHI COMMUNITIES AND TRADITIONAL WOOL TEXTILE ARTISANS
TEXTILE TYPE
WOOL TIE DYED TEXTILE
MATERIAL
SHEEP/LAMB WOOL WITH THREAD OR CORD USED FOR TYING
TECHNIQUE
RESIST TIE DYEING, SECTIONS OF WOOL ARE PINCHED AND TIGHTLY TIED BEFORE DYEING
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
DOTS, CIRCLES, SPIRAL PATTERNS, CROSS SHAPED DESIGNS
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
THIGMA IS AN OLD LADAKHI TRADITION. SIMILAR PATTERN APPEARS IN WALL PAINTINGS OF ALCHI MONASTERY, DATING TO THE 12-13th CENTURIES SUGGESTING A LONG HISTORY IN THE REGION.
TRANSFORMATION
TRADITIONALLY NATURAL DYES WERE USED BUT SYNTHETIC DYES ARE INCREASINGLY USED.
CURRENT STATUS
DECLINING/REQUIRING SAFEGUARDING IT IS IDENTIFIED AMONG INDIAN TEXTILES CRAFTS NEEDING SAFEGUARDING. IT ALSO RECEIVED GI REGISTRATION AS LADAKH THIGMA IN 2026
THREATS/CHALLENGES
VERY FEW PRACTITIONERS LABOUR INTENSIVE PRODUCTION CHANGING CLOTHING PREFERENCES, DECLINING DEMAND FOR TRADITIONAL TEXTILES AND LOSS OF TRADITIONAL DYEING PRACTICES.

AWADH JAMDANI

REGION OF ORIGIN
VARANASI, UTTAR PRADESH
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
TRADITIONAL WEAVER FAMILIES OF VARANASI
TEXTILE TYPE
FINE COTTON WOVEN TEXTILE, LOOM ENGINEERED JAMDANI
MATERIAL
FINE COTTON YARN WITH COLOURED OR METALLIC ZARI YARN FOR EXTRA WEFT PATTERNS
TECHNIQUE
INTRICATE LOOM ENGINEERED WEAVING WHERE PATTERNS ARE CREATED DIRECTLY WHILE WEAVING THE FABRIC
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
FLORAL PATTERNS, FLOWERS AND KALKA MOTIFS
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Awadh Jamdani developed under the patronage of the Nawabs of Awadh and rulers of Varanasi. It was particularly used to create tailored garments such as angarkhas.
TRANSFORMATION
After the decline of Mughal and nawabi patronage, production greatly contracted. Today, Jamdani saris continue to be woven, while the specialised Awadh Jamdani garments and accessories have become increasingly rare.
CURRENT STATUS
Rare and requiring safeguarding only a small number of skilled weavers continue the specialised tradition
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Decline in traditional patronage, fewer skilled practitioners, lengthy production process, and inadequate payment for the skill and labour involved.

BALUCHARI WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
BALUCHAR, MURSHIDABAD, WEST BENGAL
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional silk weavers of Bengal, particularly Bishnupur
TEXTILE TYPE
HANDWOVEN SILK SAREE
MATERIAL
SILK
TECHNIQUE
Traditionally woven on the jala system later Jacquard looms were adopted for complex designs.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Ramayana and Mahabharata scenes, Krishna, kings, courtesans, animals, palkis and floral design
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Baluchari originated in Baluchar and later shifted to Bishnupur after flooding. It flourished under royal patronage but declined during the colonial period
TRANSFORMATION
The tradition was revived in the 20th century, with Jacquard technology replacing the slower jala method. This increased production but also changed the intricacy and character of some designs
CURRENT STATUS
Revived but vulnerable, with the number of traditional weavers and looms having declined at different periods.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
High production costs, changing demand, competition from cheaper textiles, dependence on middlemen, low artisan wages and difficulty maintaining highly skilled traditional weaving.

ASHAVALI SARI WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
AHMEDABAD, GUJARAT
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditionally woven by Khatri and Patel weavers today practiced by a small number of specialist weavers particularly around Ridrol near Ahmedabad.
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven silk brocade sari, traditionally associated with Kinkhwab or “cloth of gold.”
MATERIAL
Mulberry silk with zari (metallic gold/silver or imitation metallic yarns).
TECHNIQUE
Traditionally woven on pit looms using twill weaving Jacquard technology later replaced the older jala system for complex patterns.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Parrots, peacocks, paisleys, flowers, animals and human figures jaal, jangla, mor popat and sikka butti are notable pattern forms
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
The tradition dates back almost six centuries and takes its name from Ashaval, the earlier settlement of Ahmedabad. Ashavali textiles were used by royalty and nobility as saris, patkas, canopies and ceremonial textiles
TRANSFORMATION
The craft moved from royal and elite patronage toward a smaller commercial market. The introduction of Jacquard looms increased the possibilities for pattern production, while contemporary designers have adapted motifs, materials and softness for modern consumers.
CURRENT STATUS
Safeguarding concern / endangered traditional practice. UNESCO identifies Ashavali sari weaving among Indian textile crafts requiring safeguarding only a limited number of specialist weavers continue the tradition
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Reduced demand for expensive handwoven saris, limited numbers of new weavers, labour intensive production and competition from other festive textiles threaten continuity. Revival efforts are focusing on contemporary designs and wider markets.

KUSTI WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Navsari and Bharuch, Gujarat
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Parsi / Zoroastrian community
TEXTILE TYPE
Sacred woven girdle
MATERIAL
Lamb’s wool or white camel hair
TECHNIQUE
Hand-spinning and weaving on a small, specialised wooden loom called a jantar
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Primarily structured thread patterns the significance lies more in the sacred construction and number of threads than decorative motifs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Kusti is a sacred cord worn by Zoroastrians. In India, Navsari became an important centre for its traditional weaving. The craft was once taught widely among Parsi girls.
TRANSFORMATION
Once a common skill among Parsi women, Kusti weaving has become a specialised practice mainly maintained by elderly women. The tradition is now being documented and revived through heritage initiatives.
CURRENT STATUS
Declining and requiring safeguarding only a limited number of skilled practitioners continue the tradition.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining Parsi population, very few skilled weavers, specialised equipment, and weakening transmission of traditional weaving knowledge.

MASHRU WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Gujarat, especially Ahmedabad and Patan
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Muslim and Hindu weaving communities of Gujarat
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven mixed-fibre fabric
MATERIAL
Silk and cotton historically silk was used on the surface with cotton forming the inner side
TECHNIQUE
Handloom weaving using a combination of silk and cotton yarns, creating a smooth, lustrous surface with a more comfortable cotton underside
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Stripes, checks, geometric patterns, and small repeated floral or ornamental designs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Mashru developed as a mixed textile in western India and was particularly associated with Gujarat. Its silk-cotton construction made it an important fabric for clothing and ceremonial use. It is documented as one of the early forms of mixed-fibre cloth.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditional Mashru production declined as industrially produced fabrics and changing clothing preferences reduced demand. Contemporary production has adapted the fabric for fashion, garments and lifestyle products.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional Mashru weaving is declining and requires safeguarding, with production surviving in limited weaving centres.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining demand for traditional fabrics, competition from cheaper machine-made textiles, reduced number of skilled weavers, changing consumer preferences, and difficulty sustaining labour intensive handloom production.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles

MATA-NI-PACHEDI

REGION OF ORIGIN
Gujarat, especially Ahmedabad
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Vaghari community traditionally made by Chitara artisan families
TEXTILE TYPE
Painted and hand-block-printed ritual textile
MATERIAL
Cotton cloth, natural dyes, mordants and wooden blocks
TECHNIQUE
Freehand painting combined with hand block-printing, dyeing and washing
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Goddess figures, animals, worshippers, floral elements and mythological/narrative scenes
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Mata-ni-Pachedi originated in Gujarat about 500 years ago as a portable sacred textile used by the Vaghari community for worship during festivals such as NavratrI
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally made as sacred cloths for Vaghari ritual use, it is now also produced for non-traditional customers as wall hangings and decorative artworks. The colour palette and designs have also expanded.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional craft requiring safeguarding only a few Chitara families continue to produce it.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining traditional commissions, limited number of skilled artisan families, changing customer base, and reduced use of the textile for its original ritual purpose
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Mata-ni-Pachedi | Gujarat”; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India.

PATOLA WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
PATAN, GUJARAT
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Salvi weaving community of Patan
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven silk sari double ikat textile
MATERIAL
Silk yarn and natural/chemical dyes
TECHNIQUE
Double ikat both warp and weft threads are resist-dyed according to the design before being woven on a specialised loom
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric patterns, flowers, parrots, elephants, dancing figures and other stylised natural and figurative motifs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Patola developed as a highly skilled weaving tradition in Patan, Gujarat. It became associated with elite and ceremonial use because of its complex technique, fine silk and elaborate designs.
TRANSFORMATION
The lengthy and highly specialised production process has made traditional Patola expensive. Changing markets and cheaper textiles have affected demand, while contemporary production has adapted designs and products for new consumers.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional craft requiring safeguarding Patola weaving is included by UNESCO among Indian textile crafts needing safeguarding.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Time consuming production, high cost, limited number of highly skilled weavers, competition from cheaper textiles and difficulty in transmitting complex double ikat knowledge to younger generations.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Patola Weaving Patan, Gujarat”; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Government of India, A Compendium of Indian Handicrafts & Handlooms covered under Geographical Indications.

ROGAN TEXTILE WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Nirona, Kutch, Gujarat
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Rogan artisan families of Kutch
TEXTILE TYPE
Painted textile / textile surface art
MATERIAL
Cotton or silk fabric, castor oil, pigments and dyes
TECHNIQUE
A thick paint made from boiled castor oil and pigments is applied to fabric using a metal stylus. The painted design may be folded onto the fabric to create a mirror-image pattern.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Tree of Life, flowers, peacocks, floral patterns and geometric designs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Rogan painting is traditionally associated with Nirona village in Kutch. Artisans trace the tradition in the area back more than 400 years. It was historically used on bridal garments and household textiles, particularly among the Ahir community.
TRANSFORMATION
Originally used for bridal clothing and household textiles, Rogan is now produced as wall hangings, cushion covers, table covers, saris and other decorative products for urban and tourist markets.
CURRENT STATUS
Rare and requiring safeguarding UNESCO records that the craft was being practised by only two families in Nirona at the time of its documentation
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Very limited number of practitioners, closely guarded specialised knowledge, weakening traditional clientele, labour Intensive production and dependence on urban/tourist markets
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Rogan Textile Painting Nirona, Gujarat”; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India, “Kutch Rogan Craft.”

SUJANI WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
BHARUCH, GUJARAT
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional weaving families of Bharuch
TEXTILE TYPE
Double cloth handwoven quilt
MATERIAL
Cotton yarn and cotton batting
TECHNIQUE
Double cloth weaving on a pit loom cotton batting is inserted between the warp and weft and enclosed within woven pockets. Two weavers operate the loom together.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Checkerboard patterns, alternating white and coloured squares, geometric designs and small floral butis
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Sujani weaving is believed to have originated in Bharuch in the mid19th century. Historical accounts connect it with fragrant quilts made using flower petals and leaves later, cotton became the principal filling material.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally produced as quilts, Sujani is now also used for bedspreads, throws, rugs and other home-textile products. Industrial blankets and changing consumer preferences have reduced demand for traditional quilts
CURRENT STATUS
Declining craft requiring safeguarding Bharuch remains the only major Sujani craft pocket in Gujarat, with only a few families practising it
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Labour intensive production, declining interest among younger generations, competition from inexpensive synthetic quilts and blankets, and the limited number of skilled weavers
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Sujani Weaving, Bharuch, Gujarat”; Ministry of Textiles, Government of India, Sustainability in the Handloom Traditions of India.

TANGALIYA WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Saurashtra region, especially Surendranagar district, Gujarat
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Dangasia community
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven woollen textile
MATERIAL
Traditionally unprocessed sheep wool cotton and other fibres are also used in contemporary products
TECHNIQUE
Beaded dot weaving, in which raised dana dots are created during weaving to form geometric patterns resembling bead embroidery
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric patterns, dots, diamonds, zigzags, stripes and stylised floral forms
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Tangaliya is an approximately 700 year old weaving tradition associated with the Dangasia community of Saurashtra. Traditionally, Dangasia weavers exchanged woven garments with Bharwaad shepherds through a barter system
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally produced for local Bharwaad consumption as head mantles, shawls and gathered skirts. Since around the 2010s, it has entered urban and commercial markets and is now adapted into contemporary garments and home textile products
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional craft requiring safeguarding the traditional handwoven form has been under pressure from changing markets and reduced demand
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining traditional demand, competition from machine made textiles, changing consumer preferences, reduced use of sheep wool, and the need to sustain specialised weaving knowledge.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles,“Tangaliya Weaving

NANDANA HAND BLOCK PRINTING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Tarapur and Ummedpura, Jawad area, Madhya Pradesh
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Chhippa printing community traditionally produced for Bhil, Bhilala, Mina and Patelia communities
TEXTILE TYPE
Hand block-printed cotton textile
MATERIAL
Cotton fabric, natural dyes, mordants and wax/resist materials
TECHNIQUE
Wax-resist hand block printing combined with repeated dyeing, resist application, washing and drying. Traditionally, indigo, madder/alizarin and other natural dyes were used.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Champakali, Mirchi (chilli), Amba (mango), Jalam/Jalam Buta (creeper pattern) and Dholamaru
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Nandana developed as a traditional resist-printing craft in the Jawad region. Its printed textiles were widely used by tribal communities for clothing, household textiles and ceremonial purposes
TRANSFORMATION
From being produced mainly for local tribal consumers, Nandana has increasingly entered urban markets. Traditional natural dyes have also been replaced partly by chemical dyes, while contemporary products include saris, garments and home textiles
CURRENT STATUS
Languishing and requiring safeguarding UNESCO reported that only three families had workshops for traditional Nandana printing in 2022
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Competition from inexpensive mill-printed textiles, changing clothing preferences, declining traditional clientele, labour intensive production, reduced access to flowing water for processing and a very small number of practising artisan families.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Nandana Hand Block-Printing

HIMROO WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Aurangabad (Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar), Maharashtra
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Himroo weaver families of Aurangabad
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven silk cotton textile brocade
MATERIAL
Cotton and silk yarn historically developed as an alternative to gold and silver rich Kinkhwab
TECHNIQUE
Extra weft tapestry style weaving on traditional pit or khada looms traditional draw loom weaving required two weavers.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Floral patterns, geometric designs, Persian inspired motifs, peacocks, paisleys and designs influenced by Ajanta art
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Himroo is associated with the Deccan and is traditionally linked to the period when Muhammad bin Tughlaq shifted his capital to Daulatabad in the 14th century. It later flourished under Mughal and Nizam patronage.
TRANSFORMATION
Himroo was once a luxury textile used by royal and elite households. After the decline of Nizam court patronage in the 20th century, demand decreased and many weavers moved to other occupations. Machine made textiles also affected traditional handloom production.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional handwoven Himroo is declining and requires safeguarding, although a small number of artisans continue the craft in Aurangabad.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining traditional patronage, competition from machine-made textiles, fewer skilled weavers, time-consuming handloom production and difficulty sustaining traditional weaving knowledge.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Himroo Weaving

JAMDANI

REGION OF ORIGIN
WEST BENGAL
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Muslim and Hindu weaver communities of Bengal
TEXTILE TYPE
Fine handwoven cotton textile / sari
MATERIAL
Fine cotton yarn silk and zari may also be used in contemporary varieties
TECHNIQUE
Handloom weaving with supplementary weft. Motifs are inserted individually into the ground fabric using a small shuttle or needle like tool
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Floral butis, paisleys, leaves, geometric patterns, vines and stylised natural forms
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Jamdani is a historic fine-muslin weaving tradition of Bengal. It developed under Mughal patronage and became particularly associated with Dhaka and Bengal's skilled weaving communities.
TRANSFORMATION
Colonial economic changes and competition from industrially produced textiles contributed to the decline of traditional fine weaving. After independence, Jamdani was revived through government, institutional and artisan initiatives and adapted to contemporary sari and fashion markets.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional handloom craft requiring safeguarding Jamdani continues to be produced but faces pressure from cheaper machine made imitations and declining skilled labour.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Time consuming production, high cost, competition from powerloom products, declining numbers of skilled weavers, changing consumer preferences and loss of traditional weaving knowledge.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Jamdani Weaving

NAVALGUND DURRIES

REGION OF ORIGIN
Navalgund, Dharwad district, Karnataka
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional women weavers and artisan families of Navalgund
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven cotton floor covering / durry
MATERIAL
Cotton yarn
TECHNIQUE
Hand weaving on traditional pit looms using the discontinuous supplementary weft technique to create intricate patterns.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric designs, birds, animals, flowers, stylised leaves and traditional chittara patterns
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Navalgund Durries have a long weaving tradition in northern Karnataka. The craft is particularly associated with women weavers and was traditionally practised as a household based occupation
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally produced mainly as floor coverings for local households, Navalgund Durries have been adapted for contemporary interiors and urban markets. Changes in lifestyle and the availability of machine made floor coverings have affected traditional production.
CURRENT STATUS
Languishing craft requiring safeguarding, with the traditional weaving community facing difficulties in sustaining the practice
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Competition from machine-made carpets and synthetic floor coverings, declining demand, low returns for artisans, labour intensive weaving and reduced transmission of skills to younger generations.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Navalgund Durries

CHANDERI

Chanderi, Madhya Pradesh
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Chanderi weaver families and handloom communities
TEXTILE TYPE
Fine handwoven sari and dress fabric
MATERIAL
Silk and cotton yarn; zari is used for decorative borders and motifs
TECHNIQUE
Handloom weaving using a fine-count yarn, often combining a silk warp with cotton weft to create Chanderi's characteristic lightweight and translucent fabric
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Ashrafi (coin), peacocks, flowers, leaves, geometric patterns and traditional butis
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Chanderi has a long history of fine textile production in central India. Its weaving tradition received patronage from royal and aristocratic communities and became known for its lightweight, delicate and translucent fabrics.
TRANSFORMATION
The craft moved from royal and elite patronage toward wider commercial markets. Contemporary Chanderi includes saris, dress materials and fashion products, while mechanised production and changing consumer preferences have affected traditional handloom w
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional handloom craft requiring safeguarding; Chanderi continues to have an active weaving community but faces economic and technological pressures.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Competition from powerloom and synthetic fabrics, rising production costs, declining availability of skilled weavers, changing consumer preferences and difficulties in sustaining traditional handloom livelihoods.

PASHMINA /KANI WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Kashmir Valley, Jammu & Kashmir
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Traditional Kashmiri Kani weavers and Pashmina artisans
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven fine woollen shawl
MATERIAL
Fine Pashmina wool from Himalayan goats; silk or cotton may be used for some supplementary elements
TECHNIQUE
Kani weaving uses small wooden kanis (twill-tapestry sticks) to insert coloured threads and create intricate patterns while weaving.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Paisley/buta, flowers, leaves, vines, chinar leaves, geometric forms and elaborate jaldar patterns
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Kani weaving is an old Kashmiri textile tradition associated with the production of luxurious shawls. It flourished under royal and elite patronage, particularly during the Mughal period.
TRANSFORMATION
Decline of royal patronage and changing European and Indian markets affected traditional Kani shawl production. Contemporary artisans have adapted designs and products to meet modern demand, while machine-made imitations have increased competition.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional craft requiring safeguarding; authentic handwoven Kani-Pashmina remains highly specialised and time-consuming.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Long production time, high cost of authentic Pashmina, declining numbers of highly skilled weavers, competition from machine-made imitations, changing consumer preferences and difficulty transmitting specialised Kani weaving knowledge.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles, “Kani Weaving | Kashmir”; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India.

NAGA TRADITIONAL TEXTILES

REGION OF ORIGIN
Nagaland and adjoining Naga-inhabited areas of Northeast India
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Naga tribal communities, including Ao, Angami, Lotha, Sumi, Chakhesang and other Naga groups
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven cotton textiles, shawls, wraps and traditional garments
MATERIAL
Cotton yarn wool and synthetic yarns are also used in contemporary production
TECHNIQUE
Handloom weaving on traditional loin looms/backstrap looms, with supplementary-weft and other patterning techniques depending on the community
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric bands, diamonds, stripes, zigzags, animal figures and community specific symbolic patterns
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Naga textiles are closely connected with social identity, community traditions and ceremonial life. Different Naga groups developed distinctive patterns and colours that could indicate social status, clan or achievements.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditional textiles once produced primarily for community use are increasingly made for urban and commercial markets. Contemporary products include shawls, bags, clothing and home furnishings, while some traditional patterns and materials have been modified for modern consumers
CURRENT STATUS
Living traditional craft, but several traditional weaving practices require safeguarding and continued transmission.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Changing lifestyles, declining use of traditional garments, reduced transmission of weaving knowledge, competition from machine-made textiles and commercial adaptation of traditional designs.
SOURCE

TODA EMBROIDERY

REGION OF ORIGIN
Nilgiri Hills, Tamil Nadu
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Toda tribal community
TEXTILE TYPE
Hand-embroidered cotton textile / shawl
MATERIAL
White cotton cloth and coloured wool or cotton threads
TECHNIQUE
Hand embroidery using a counting technique on the fabric surface. Red and black threads are traditionally worked to create dense geometric patterns.
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric forms, diamonds, triangles, zigzags, parallel lines and stylised representations of natural and sacred elements
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Toda embroidery is a distinctive textile tradition of the Toda people of the Nilgiri Hills. Embroidered shawls, known as poothkuli, have traditionally been worn during ceremonial and everyday occasions.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally produced mainly for community use, Toda embroidery has increasingly entered outside markets through shawls, bags, garments and decorative products. This has created new income opportunities but has also changed designs and production practices.
CURRENT STATUS
Living traditional craft requiring safeguarding and continued transmission within the Toda community.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining traditional use, changing lifestyles, reduced transmission of skills, competition from machine-made imitations and the need to maintain traditional motifs and techniques while meeting commercial demand.

LAMBADI/BANJARA TEXTILES

REGION OF ORIGIN
Rajasthan and Gujarat; now practiced by Banjara/Lambadi communities across several parts of India
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Lambadi / Banjara tribal community
TEXTILE TYPE
Embroidered traditional clothing and decorative textiles
MATERIAL
Cotton fabric, coloured threads, mirrors, shells, beads, coins and metal ornaments
TECHNIQUE
Hand embroidery using counted-thread stitches, chain stitch, cross-stitch, appliqué and mirror work
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric patterns, triangles, diamonds, squares, flowers, animals and symbolic motifs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Lambadi/Banjara textiles are closely connected with the community's nomadic history, identity and dress traditions. Elaborately embroidered garments, particularly women's ghagras, blouses and odhnis, traditionally expressed social and cultural identity.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditional embroidered clothing has increasingly been adapted into bags, wall hangings, cushion covers, accessories and contemporary garments for urban and tourist markets.
CURRENT STATUS
Living traditional textile tradition, but traditional forms and knowledge require continued safeguarding
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Changing lifestyles, declining use of traditional dress, loss of specialised embroidery knowledge, competition from machine-made embroidery and commercial adaptation of traditional designs.

UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India, documentation on Lambadi/Banjara embroidery and textiles.

KOTPAD WEAVING

REGION OF ORIGIN
Kotpad, Koraput district, Odisha
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Mirgan weaving community, traditionally associated with the Dongria Kondh and other tribal communities
TEXTILE TYPE
Handwoven cotton textile
MATERIAL
Locally grown cotton yarn and natural vegetable dyes, especially dye from aal (Morinda citrifolia) roots
TECHNIQUE
Handloom weaving combined with traditional natural dyeing. The yarn is repeatedly dyed and treated with natural materials to produce the characteristic deep reddish-brown colour
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Geometric patterns, stripes, checks, lines and simple tribal-inspired motifs
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Kotpad weaving is a traditional textile craft of Koraput, Odisha. Its distinctive natural dyeing tradition developed alongside the weaving practices of local artisan communities and has long been connected with tribal markets and dress
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally produced for tribal communities, Kotpad textiles have entered wider markets through contemporary saris, shawls, stoles and home products. Natural dyeing has remained an important identity of the craft, although production and market conditions have changed.
CURRENT STATUS
Traditional craft requiring safeguarding, with a limited number of artisans continuing the specialised weaving and natural-dyeing practices.
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Declining availability of skilled artisans, time-consuming natural dyeing, limited supply of suitable raw materials, competition from cheaper textiles, changing consumer preferences and difficulty sustaining artisan livelihoods.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India, documentation on Kotpad Handloom.

PHULKARI EMBROIDERY

REGION OF ORIGIN
Punjab, especially Punjab region of North India
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Punjabi women and traditional artisan communities
TEXTILE TYPE
Hand-embroidered textile / shawl / dupatta
MATERIAL
Handspun cotton cloth (khaddar) and untwisted silk floss thread (pat) contemporary pieces may use other fabric
TECHNIQUE
Surface embroidery using counted-thread darning stitch, worked mainly from the reverse side of the fabric
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Flowers, leaves, geometric forms, birds, animals and stylised plants
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Phulkari is a traditional embroidery practice of Punjab, historically passed from mother to daughter. Embroidered odhnis, dupattas and shawls were important parts of women's clothing and ceremonial traditions.
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally created for household and ceremonial use, Phulkari declined during the 20th century as lifestyles and clothing practices changed. It has since been revived through cooperatives, government initiatives and commercial fashion and is now used on garments, bags and home décor
CURRENT STATUS
Living traditional craft requiring safeguarding and continued transmission of traditional embroidery skills
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Decline of traditional hand embroidery, reduced transmission to younger generations, machine-made imitations, changing consumer preferences and commercial production that may simplify traditional designs and techniques.
SOURCE
Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India; UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles.

KANTHA EMBROIDERY

REGION OF ORIGIN
Bengal, especially West Bengal and Bangladesh
COMMUNITY/ PRACTITIONERS
Rural Bengali women and traditional artisan communities
TEXTILE TYPE
Embroidered quilt / household textile
MATERIAL
Recycled cotton saris or cloth layers and cotton thread
TECHNIQUE
Running stitch embroidery used to join layers of old cloth. Dense stitching creates textured surfaces and decorative patterns
MOTIFS/ DESIGN
Flowers, trees, birds, animals, fish, geometric patterns, human figures and mythological or everyday scenes
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Kantha developed as a domestic textile tradition in Bengal, where women reused old saris and cloth by layering and stitching them together. It served both practical and ceremonial purposes
TRANSFORMATION
Traditionally made for household use from recycled clothing, Kantha has moved into commercial markets as saris, shawls, bags, quilts, wall hangings and fashion products. Contemporary designs may use new fabrics and more elaborate embroidery
CURRENT STATUS
Living traditional craft with continued production, but traditional domestic forms and techniques require safeguarding
THREATS/CHALLENGES
Commercialisation, machine-made imitations, changing lifestyles, reduced use of traditional household textiles and loss of traditional knowledge among younger generations.
SOURCE
UNESCO, Handmade for the 21st Century: Safeguarding Traditional Indian Textiles; Development Commissioner (Handicrafts), Ministry of Textiles, Government of India, documentation on Kantha Embroidery.
